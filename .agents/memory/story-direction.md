---
name: Story direction
description: User's requested cultural setting, language simplicity, and narration direction for StoryBowl.
---
StoryBowl stories should have an Indian backdrop, using normal language that is easy to understand. Keep English narration unless the user requests another language.

**Why:** The user asked to tune stories for an Indian backdrop and easy language. They found the browser voice too robotic and asked for research into free natural storytelling APIs.

**How to apply:** Preserve this direction when editing story prompts or demo examples. Prefer everyday, varied Indian settings over stereotypes. Do not assume the user approved a specific hosted voice provider merely because they requested research.

Stories should be roughly 200–300 words in 3–4 short paragraphs, with a proper title, a setup, a twist, and a payoff. Every chosen object must play a meaningful role. Refer to selectable items as "objects" everywhere, never "ingredients". Preserve the warm, clean light-colour style, with bright colour only where needed, and do not use the oval object-collection shape.

**Why:** The user superseded the earlier shorter-story requirement during the course-submission improvement pass, requested a meaningful role for every object, and explicitly asked to preserve the existing warm, clean style.

**How to apply:** Carry these requirements into future writing, UI changes, accessibility labels, error messages, and demos. The user chose to keep browser voices; do not add a hosted narrator without a new request.

The UI is for a general audience: do not specify India, Indian, or a region in interface copy. Remove unnecessary language, word-count, and listening-duration tags; keep the page natural, light, and easy to navigate.

**Why:** The user explicitly separated general-audience UI wording from story-generation direction and rejected the "English · 100–150 words · about 45–70 seconds" tag.

**How to apply:** Keep the existing story backdrop and length rules behind the scenes, not as promotional labels or badges. Generated story text may still name its setting; this instruction concerns the interface.

Inspect actual generated stories before claiming narrative realism, especially at ten selected objects. Prompt instructions and valid word counts do not establish a believable plot.

**Why:** Live ten-object samples sometimes became fantastical or gave weak explanations despite explicit realism instructions and plain-language examples.

**How to apply:** Review cause and effect, natural object use, and whether a mystery resolves its original question. Be clear about model-dependent quality rather than treating structural validation as a writing-quality check.

Do not promote a stronger realism prompt or switch models on wording alone. Keep unsuccessful prompt experiments out of the running app, preserve their measured examples, and seek approval before a model comparison or switch.

**Why:** Controlled pilots with explicit prop grounding and a separate structured-fictional-facts approach still produced contradictory keys, animal messengers and weak mystery explanations. Neither established a useful quality gain.

**How to apply:** Compare actual prose with the same human rubric and controlled inputs before claiming improvement. Treat the small pilot as evidence of risks, not a guarantee that a particular model always fails or succeeds.
